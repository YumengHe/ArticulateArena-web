A reconstructed cabinet should have the right shape, move correctly, and look like the original.

Different metrics check these different properties. Here, reference means the correct object, and prediction means the reconstructed model we want to evaluate.

Compare the shape

Imagine placing dots all over both objects’ surfaces. If the shapes agree, the dots on one object should be close to dots on the other.

Chamfer distance (CD)

For each dot, measure the gap to the nearest dot on the other object. Average these gaps in both directions, so missing pieces also matter.

Lower is better. Zero means the two sets of sampled dots coincide. A missing handle or a misplaced surface can increase the distance.

F-score

Two dots count as a match when their distance is no more than an allowed gap. F-score combines two checks: how much of the prediction matches, and how much of the reference it covers.

Higher is better. A score of 100% means all sampled dots have a match within the allowed gap. The shapes can still differ slightly.

Check how the parts move

A joint connects two parts and lets them move relative to each other. A door hinge lets the door rotate; a drawer joint lets the drawer slide. These four errors check different aspects of that movement.

Axis error (AxisErr)

Checks the direction of the joint. A door hinge should be vertical; predicting it at a 15° tilt gives a 15° axis error. Lower is better.

Pivot error (PivotErr)

Checks the location of the hinge. Here it points in the right direction but sits 5 cm away from the correct line. Lower is better.

Type error (TypeErr)

Checks the kind of movement. A drawer should slide; a rotating hinge is the wrong type. Often reported as the percentage of joints with the wrong type. Lower is better.

Limit error (Limit Err.)

Checks how far the part can move. This door should open to 90°, but stops at 45°. An error rate counts joints whose limits fail the chosen tolerance. Lower is better.

Compare how it looks

Take a picture of the reconstructed model from the same viewpoint as the reference image. These metrics compare the two pictures, including their visible shape, color, and texture.

The outline is unchanged, but the door has a different finish. Image similarity can change even when the geometry agrees.

LPIPS

Compares visual details using features learned by an image model. Changes to edges, textures, or colors can affect it. Lower means more visually similar.

CLIP similarity

Compares the images’ overall content. Two different cabinets can score well because both depict a cabinet. Higher means more similar.

DINOv2 similarity

Compares learned visual features of the two images, which can capture object shape and parts. Higher means more similar.

None of these scores proves that the door opens correctly or that hidden parts exist. Use the same camera and lighting when you want to compare the models rather than their rendering conditions.

Check for missing joints

A cabinet should have a rotating door and a sliding drawer. The prediction gets the door right but leaves the drawer fixed. The door’s error can be zero, even though the drawer’s sliding joint is missing.

  • Matched: a corresponding joint was found, such as the door hinge. Its accuracy still needs to be checked.
  • Missing: an expected joint was left out, such as the drawer’s sliding joint.
  • Extra: a joint was added where none should exist, such as making the fixed cabinet body rotate.
